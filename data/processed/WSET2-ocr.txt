Introduction
Every year there are new crops of grapes and new wines to discover and enjoy.
Each vintage is different but there are certain things that remain true, and this means that grape varieties and the wines made from them are recognisable from one year to the next,
The WSET Level 2 Award in Wines focuses on these constants. How are grapes grown? How is wine made? How do sparkling wines get their bubbles? Above all else, though, this qualification looks at grape varieties and what they contribute to individual wines that are found throughout the world. Some, such as Cabernet
Sauvignon and Chardonnay, are grown successfully in nearly every wine-producing country. Others, such as Albarific and Corvina, are local specialities that give the world of wine the variety that is such fun to explore.
This book has been written to help you understand how the grape variety, growing environment and winemaking choices influence the styles of wines produced around the world. We hope that you enjoy reading it and discovering more about the fascinating world of wine.
We wish you every success with your WSET studies and hope your thirst for knowledge continues to grow as you delve deeper into the fascinating world of wine.

Writing tasting notes helps you to communicate to other people what a wine is like, sometimes long after you have tasted it.
You may wish to use the notes to remind yourself of a wine you liked, to compare it with another wine or to make a recommendation. The WSET Level 2 Award in

Preparing for tasting
An ideal tasting environment will have good lighting for judging the wine's appearance. It will be free of strong odours to avoid interference with the wine's aromas. It will also have sufficient space for you to lay out your wine glasses and make notes. Spittoons or spit cups should be available,
To prepare yourself to assess wines you need to make sure that you have a clean palate, free from the lingering flavours of toothpaste or strongly flavoured foods.
You also need to be considerate of others so avoid wearing perfumes, aftershaves or other strongly scented products as these will interfere with the wine aromas.
You will also need suitable glassware. It should be odourless, colourless, transparent and free of any residues, such as detergent or dishwasher salts, or dirt left from unclean glass-polishing cloths. The ISO glass is suitable for evaluating wines. The two important features are the rounded bowl, which aids with swirling the wine to release aromas, and the inward-sloping walls, which capture these aromas at the top of the glass. There are many other glasses that are suitable for tasting, but they all share these two features and are small enough to use with a tasting sample of wine, rather than requiring a larger sample.

When pouring your samples, you should try to ensure you consistently pour the same volume into each glass. We suggest a 5 cl (1.7 US fl. oz.) sample. This should be sufficient to assess the appearance, nose and palate of the wine, but small enough that you are able to swirl the sample in the base of the bowl without spilling it.
Appearance
Clarity
The vast majority of wines can be described as ‘clear’. Haziness is caused by particles suspended in the wine. If a wine has an unusually high amount of suspended particles then it can be described as ‘hazy’. This may indicate a fault but the precise nature of the fault can normally only be determined by assessing the wine on the nose and palate. Alternatively, it can sometimes indicate that the wine has not been clarified before packaging (see Chapter 5 for more information on wine clarification).
Intensity
Intensity is how much colour the wine has. The level of intensity can be assessed by holding the glass at a 45° angle and looking through the liquid from above to see how far the colour extends from the core, the deepest part of the bowl, to the rim where there is the shallowest depth of wine. For red wines, it can also be assessed by looking down through an upright glass. In this instance, look at the point where the stem of the glass is attached to the bowl and assess how easily the stem can be seen.
All white wines appear colourless right at the rim when the glass is held at a 45°
angle. A white wine that has a broad watery rim should be described as 'pale’, if the pigment reaches almost to the rim it should be described as ‘deep’. For red wines, hold the glass at an angle and look at the rim; if the wine is lightly pigmented from the rim to the core, it can be described as ‘pale’. In this instance, when looking through an upright glass, it should be easy to clearly see the stem of the glass. If the wine is intensely pigmented right up to the rim, it should be described as ‘deep’; looking down through the wine in the bowl, it should be impossible to see the stem.
Colour
When assessing colour, white wines can be placed on a scale that runs from
‘lemon-green’ to ‘brown’. The most common colour for white wines is ‘lemon’. If there is a noticeable greenness to the colour, the wine is ‘lemon-green’, If there is a hint of orange or brown, the wine is ‘gold’. Wines with a very noticeable level of browning could be described as "amber or ‘brown’.

Red wines can be placed on a scale that runs from "purple’ through to ‘brown’. The most common colour for red wine is ‘ruby’. Wines with a noticeable blue or purple colour are described as ‘purple’. If there is a noticeable orange or brown colour but the wine is still more red than brown, it is described as ‘garnet’. If the wine is more brown than red, it may be described as ‘tawny’. ‘Brown’ should be used for wines where no redness in the colour remains.
Rosé wines have their own set of colour descriptions. ‘Pink’ describes wines that have a very pure pink colour. If a pink-coloured wine shows a hint of orange, then it can be described as ‘pink-orange’. ‘Orange’ can be used to describe a rose in which orange is the dominant colour. These wines are very rare,
Nose
A large part of the pleasure to be gained from tasting wine comes from the aromas.
The differences in character and the clarity of the aromas account for much of the difference between basic-quality and very fine wines.
To assess the nose, you should swirl the liquid to release the aromas into the glass.
Then, place your nose over the rim of the glass and take a short sniff, noting the intensity and the detailed characteristics of the aromas you find. Some aromas are very delicate, and you may gain some insight into the wine by taking a quick sniff before swirling.
Condition
The first thing to note is whether the wine is showing any faults. Common sources of wine faults and their impact on wines are described in Chapter 3. If the wine smells of damp cardboard; honey, caramel or coffee (when that is not the expected style of the wine); or is lacking freshness and its expected fruit character it is likely that the wine is faulty.
aroma intensity
As a general rule, if the aromas are immediately apparent when you insert your nose into the glass then they are ‘pronounced’. If, even after swirling, you find the aromas to be faint and hard to detect, the intensity is ‘light’. Otherwise, it falls into the ‘medium’ category.
aroma characteristics
Describing a wine's aroma can be a challenging task. The Wine-Lexicon has been designed to help with this part of writing a tasting note by offering a structured approach to aroma identification and description.

There are three main types of aromas: primary aromas, secondary aromas and tertiary aromas. The Wine-Lexicon is broken down into sections to reflect this.
Each aroma type is subdivided into individual clusters, for example, ‘Citrus fruit’,
‘Black fruit’ and ‘Herbaceous’, which each include a number of descriptors, such as ‘grapefruit’, ‘lemon’, ‘lime’, etc. If you work through the aromas systematically and ask yourself questions about the kind of aromas you are smelling, you will be less likely to miss something important. Note that not every wine has primary, secondary and tertiary aromas.
Primary aromas
These are the aromas that come from the grapes or are created during the fermentation process.
A simple wine may show a very limited number of primary aromas, often all within the same cluster. A more complex wine may display many more primary aromas in a range of clusters. The vast majority of wines display fruity aromas, but you should always consider whether a wine purely has fruit aromas or whether aromas from the floral, herbaceous or other primary clusters are present.
Secondary aromas
These aromas are created by post-fermentation winemaking. The most obvious of these are aromas extracted from ocak, such as vanilla and smoke. Secondary aromas also include cream and butter characteristics from malolactic conversion or the toasted-bread and biscuit aromas that can develop as a result of autolysis in sparkling wines.
Tertiary aromas
These aromas have their origin in the ageing processes. The ageing process could be oxidative (caused by the action of oxygen), for example, due to a long period in oak. This can add tertiary aromas such as coffee or caramel. Alternatively, the ageing process could be protected from the action of oxygen, for example, due to a long period in-bottle. This can add tertiary aromas such as petrol
(gasoline), honey or mushroom.
In both instances, the ageing process changes the primary aromas. In particular, fruit aromas become less fresh and can take on a dried-fruit character.

Palate
There are many different components to be considered on the palate and it is often necessary to take more than one sip of wine in order to assess it fully.
Sweetness
Sweetness is the taste of sugars present in the wine. A ‘dry’ wine has no sugar or has levels that are so low that they cannot be detected by the tongue. If the wine has a tiny amount of detectable sugar, the wine is described as ‘off-dry".
‘Medium’ covers wines with a distinct presence of sugar, but which are generally not sweet enough to partner most desserts.
‘Sweet’ covers wines where the presence of sugar has become the prominent feature of the wine. This rather broad category covers most classic sweet wines such as Sauternes and Port.
acidity
All wines contain acid and, when tasting wines, you are judging how much acidity they have. Even wines with low acidity still have some noticeable acidity and highacid wines are not as high in acidity as some acidic foods such as vinegar.
Acid is detected on the tongue, where it causes a tingling sensation and makes your mouth water. The higher the level of acid in the wine, the more your mouth waters and the longer it waters for.
When considering the acidity in a wine there are two points to remember. First, high levels of sugar and acid can mask each other. In a sweet wine, the high acidity is not the single predominant feature; rather, it serves to balance with the high sugar levels. therefore, the acidity in a sweet wine appears less obvious compared with the acidity in a dry high-acid wine such as a Chablis. However, whatever the level of sugar, the mouth-watering effect caused by acidity remains and this is always a reliable guide when it comes to judging the level of acidity. Second, alcohol can create a burning sensation that feels similar to acidity. Again, consider the mouthwatering effect to see whether this is due to acidity or alcohol for any particular wine, tannin tannins are an important structural component in red wines. They are mostly extracted from the skins of the grapes during fermentation (see section Making dry red wines in Chapter 5). tannins bind to your saliva and cause your mouth to dry up and feel rough. tannins can sometimes also have a bitter taste that lingers at the back of your mouth.

alcohol alcohol contributes to the ‘body’ of a wine. High levels of alcohol can make a wine seem heavier in the mouth. At low levels, the wine can seem a bit watery, unless there is another component such as sugar to give the wine body.
At high levels, alcohol triggers pain receptors, giving a hot, burning sensation, especially after spitting or swallowing.
When using the SAT, alcohol levels in wine are judged using the three-point scale:
« low: below 11% abv
« medium: 11-13.9% abv
« high: 14% abv and above.
The alcohol levels for fortified wines are also split into three:
+ low: 15-16.4% abv

« high: 18.5% abv and above.
Body
Body is an overall impression of how the wine feels in the mouth using the sense of touch rather than taste. Body is not a single component: it is created by the structural components (sugar, acidity, tannin and alcohol) in a wine working together. For most wines, alcohol is the main factor contributing to body. Sugar adds to the body; high acidity makes a wine feel lighter in body. Generally, high levels of ripe tannin make a wine feel fuller-bodied, but low levels of unripe tannin can make it seem harsher, thinner and therefore lighter in body.
Flavour intensity
When judging the intensity of flavours, you are judging how powerful they are.
Generally, a wine will have the same flavour intensity as aroma intensity on the nose.
However, the warming of the wine in your mouth can make some characteristics more apparent than they were on the nose. Some spice characteristics tend to be more prominent on the palate. Floral characteristics are sometimes less prominent on the palate than they appear on the nose. Taking these factors into consideration a wine may be lighter or more pronounced in intensity on the palate.
Flavour characteristics
Generally, flavours on the palate should be the same as aromas detected on the nose. In addition to checking that you have found them, you should also note down any additional characteristics you may have detected due to the wine warming in the mouth.

Finish
The finish is the collection of sensations after you have swallowed or spat the wine out. How long the sensations linger is an important indicator of quality, but when assessing the length of the finish you should only count the persistence of the desirable sensations. A wine with a very long lingering bitterness could be described as having a bitter aftertaste, but if the fruit impressions disappear quickly, the finish should be described as ‘short’. How long the sensations last will vary from taster to taster. Generally, if the pleasant flavours disappear and a structural component such as acid is the lingering flavour on the palate, the finish is ‘short’. When the flavours last as long or beyond the structural components, the finish is described as ‘long’.
Conclusions
Quality level
Many criteria are commonly used when assessing the quality of a wine. Although there are instances when wine professionals disagree about the quality of a wine, in most cases there is broad agreement about both the quality level of a wine and the reasons for its quality level. This is possible because there are a number of criteria that are widely used when it comes to assessing quality.
Balance — In wines, balance can be thought of as a set of scales, with one element of the wine helping to balance another.
Sugar in wine can be balanced by acidity, making the wine seem more refreshing.
alcohol can seem less burning and more integrated into the wine if there is sufficient fruit flavour intensity to balance the level of alcohol. A wine with high acidity may seem thin and unpleasant if it does not have sufficient fruit flavour intensity to balance the acidity. If a wine is out of balance, it is rarely more than acceptable in quality.
Length/finish — Length refers to how long the pleasant sensations linger after the wine has been swallowed or spat out. Generally, wines that have a short finish will be assessed as lower in quality than those that have a long finish. How the wine finishes, in addition to the length, is another consideration when assessing quality.
For example, if you are left with an unpleasant burning sensation from alcohol, this would not be considered a positive quality in a wine.
Intensity — A wine that has weak, dilute flavours is seldom good quality, but beyond a certain level, more intensity does not necessarily mean higher quality. When discussing quality some people also refer to a wine's intensity as its ‘concentration’.

Complexity — Complex flavours and aromas are desirable features in many wines.
The complexity can come from the primary aromas and flavours alone, or it may come from the combination of these with secondary and tertiary characteristics.
However, the lack of a range of different flavours is not always a negative, and not all premium wines are complex. In these instances, purity and definition are what make a wine great and the presence of oak or tertiary characters might detract from the quality. Ice wines often fall into this category.
When putting a wine into a quality level, think about how positively the wines can be assessed against these four criteria. An ‘outstanding’ wine will show positively against all four criteria. A wine that is 'very good’ will show positively against three of the criteria but fall short on one. For example, it may be well balanced, with a range of different flavours of medium intensity but have a short finish. If it is just
‘good’, then the wine will show positively against two of the criteria but fall short on the other two. An ‘acceptable’ wine will show positively against one criterion but fall short on three. For example, although the wine may be balanced, it may be dilute with simple flavours and have a short finish. A ‘poor’ wine will have problems when judged on all four criteria.

Pairing Wine and Food
Food that is consumed with wine has an effect on the way you perceive a wine, and wine can also have a similar effect on food. The purpose of food and wine pairing is to take advantage of these effects, so that the food and wine consumed together ideally provide more pleasure than either would if consumed separately.
Knowledge of the ways in which food and wine interact with each other also helps to avoid negative or unpleasant combinations.
When it comes to understanding the basic taste interactions between food and wine, it is important to remember that people have different sensitivities to various flavour and aroma components. The same level of bitterness, for example, can affect one person much more strongly than another. This is different from a personal preference in that some people like strong reactions while others find them unpleasant. This variation in both individual sensitivities and preferences means that what seems like a ‘perfect pairing’ to one person may seem ordinary or even unsuccessful to another. Pairings should therefore take into account the preferences and/or sensitivities of the individual, as well as the basic interactions between food and wine,
Food and wine interactions
When you place food in your mouth your taste buds adapt so that the perception of the levels of sugar, salt, acid, etc. in what you taste next can be altered. An extreme example would be the unpleasantly acidic taste of orange juice when consumed immediately after using toothpaste. In addition to this, some foods, such as chocolate or thick creamy dishes, have a mouth-coating effect that can impair the sense of taste.
There are two components in food that tend to make wines taste ‘harder’ (more drying and bitter, more acidic, less sweet, and less fruity). These are sweetness and umami. There are also two components whose presence in food tends to make wines taste ‘softer’ (less drying and bitter, less acidic, sweeter, and more fruity).
These are salt and acid. Generally, food has more impact on the way a wine tastes than the other way around. This impact is more likely to be negative than positive.

Sweetness in food
Sweetness in a dish can make a dry wine seem less fruity and taste unpleasantly acidic. With dishes containing sugar, a good general rule is to select a wine that has a higher level of sweetness than the dish.
Umami in food
Umami is a taste that is distinct from the other primary tastes. It can be difficult to isolate because umami tends to be present with other tastes such as salt that balance the negative effects of umami. Examples of foods with umami taste are cooked or dried mushrooms, air-dried meats, aged cheeses and miso.
acidity in food acidity in food is generally a good thing for food and wine pairing because it can balance a wine with very high acidity and enhance its fruitiness. However, if the level of acid in the wine is low, high levels of acid in foods can make it seem less fruity.
Salt in food
Salt is used in cooking and there is good reason for this, because it enhances the flavour in the food. Think how tasteless food seems without salt — even foods with little flavour, like a boiled potato or rice, have less flavour when they are unsalted.
Salt can have the same effect on wine and is a wine-friendly component of food that can make the wine seem fruitier and soften the tannin in red wines.

Other considerations
Chilli heat
Chilli heat is a warming or burning sensation, and levels of sensitivity can vary greatly from person to person. There is also huge variation in how pleasant or unpleasant this effect feels to the individual. The effects caused by chilli heat are greater in wines with high alcohol levels than those with lower alcohol levels.
alcohol also increases the burning sensation of the chilli; some people enjoy this effect.
Flavour intensity
It is usually desirable for the flavour intensities of the food and wine to be matched so that one does not overpower the other.
Acid and fat
Most people find the combination of acidic wines with fatty or oily foods to be very satisfying. The pairing can provide a pleasant sensation of the acidic wine
‘cutting through’ the fattiness of the food and cleansing the palate. This effect is very subjective.

There are many traditions associated with the storage and the service of wine.

However, regardless of their history and local importance, they should all be

designed to ensure the wine is served in the best possible condition.

Storing wine

If a wine is stored incorrectly it can affect the quality of the wine. Initially, wines

can start to lose some of their character and ultimately it can result in the wine

developing faults.

The three points detailed below should be followed when storing wine.

« For long-term storage, wines should be kept at a cool and constant temperature. Extremes of cold and heat, as well as significant changes in temperature, can damage a wine.

- Keep wines away from strong sunshine and bright artificial light. The light, as well as the heat they both generate, can cause a wine to lose its expected fruit character.

« If a wine is sealed with a cork then it should be stored on its side. This ensures that the cork remains in contact with the wine and stays moist. If the cork dries out, it can let air into the bottle, which can cause the wine to oxidise and lose its fruit character. Wines sealed with a screwcap can be stored standing up.

Service of wine

Service temperature

Typically, white, rosé and sparkling wines are served chilled. Red wines are

typically served either lightly chilled or at room temperature. Recommended

service conditions are given in this chapter, but they should not be used to override personal preference. If someone prefers to drink a wine at a different temperature, then as a host you should respect their preferences.

In order to keep the wine chilled until the bottle is finished, some people use an insulated container. Others use an ice bucket. If you use an ice bucket, remember to fill it with cold water as well as ice. If the bucket is only filled with ice, it will not work as efficiently.
Do not be tempted to heat a wine, for example by placing it close to a radiator, in order to raise the temperature. This can damage the flavours of the wine. Place it in the room where it will be served and allow the temperature to rise naturally.
Glassware
There are hundreds of different designs of glasses, but one thing that all of the best glasses have in common is that the bowl of the glass narrows at the rim. This allows the aromas in the wine to be concentrated inside the glass and better appreciated.
Typically, red-wine glasses are slightly larger than white-wine glasses, and sparkling wines are served in flutes or tulip-shaped glasses.
Preparing wine glasses
Wine glasses must be completely clean before they are used. Dirt, or a residue of detergent, can spoil a wine.
Glasses should always be polished before use to ensure that any lingering dirt or finger marks are removed.
Serving wine
When serving wine, you should take the following steps to ensure that the wine looks and tastes its best.
« Check the appearance of the wine. Any haziness could indicate that the wine is faulty.
« Check there are no bits floating in the wine.
« Check the aroma of the wine for any faults. The wine should not, for example, have aromas of wet cardboard or vinegar.
Keeping a wine fresh
Once a bottle of wine has been opened, it slowly starts to become stale (lose its fresh character) as it reacts with the air. Keeping a wine in the fridge with the cork in can help to keep the wine fresher for longer. If you need to keep a partially full bottle of wine fresh for even longer, there are some simple systems that you can use to extend the life of the wine.
Vacuum systems — These systems rely on a pump to remove the air from inside the bottle.
Inert gas systems — These systems make use of gases, such as nitrogen and argon, which are called ‘inert’ because they do not react with the wine. These gases are used within the bottle to avoid the wine coming into contact with air.

Common wine faults
Before serving a wine, it is best to check that the wine is as expected. Occasionally a wine may have a fault and there are a number of causes for these faults in wine.
In this chapter are listed some of the more common reasons a fault may develop, along with descriptions of how these faults may be detected in a wine.
Cork taint
Cork taint is caused by the chemical known as TCA (Trichloroanisole). The main source of this is contaminated cork and for this reason wines affected with TCA
are referred to as ‘corked’.
The taint gives wines aromas reminiscent of damp cardboard. The fruit aromas and flavours are muted and appear less fresh.
Failure of closure
If a closure allows unwanted oxygen to interact with a wine, the wine will oxidise.
The wine will appear browner than it should be. It may have aromas of honey, caramel or coffee and will lack freshness and fruitiness. Some wines are made in an oxidative style or gain some of these characteristics after many years of bottle maturation. However, you would not expect these characteristics in the majority of wines that are drunk when they are young and fruity.
Heat damage
If a wine is stored in conditions that are too hot, in direct sunlight or under bright artificial lights, it will become damaged. The wine can lose its freshness quickly and lack the expected fruit character. Storing wine at the correct temperature and in the right conditions both for long-term and short-term storage is essential for the wine to either stay fresh or mature in-bottle.

Winemakers do not forage for grapes. Grapes for winemaking are grown in carefully managed vineyards. Grape-growing is a highly skilled job that requires the grower to control the vines in order to produce a regular crop of healthy grapes.
What are vines?
A vine is a plant that can live for 60 years or more. Every year a vine follows a similar pattern of growth. Spring marks the start of the growing season. A vine grows new shoots, leaves and flowers and by the end of the summer the flowers have transformed into a crop of ripe grapes. In the autumn the vine drops its leaves marking the end of the growing season. During the winter months, vines are dormant and do not grow.
There are a number of different species of vine that are native to either Eurasia or
North America. However, it is the one European species that is, in the vast majority of instances, used to make wines. As is common in any plant species, there are different varieties. They all belong to the same European species, but each variety has different characteristics. In the case of vines these differences affect the appearance of the grapes as well as their tastes and flavours. For example, the grape variety Riesling is not only different in colour from the variety Cabernet
Sauvignon, but these two varieties have very different flavours too. All of these differences will be explored in detail in Chapters 6 to 23.

What a vine needs
Like all plants, a vine needs certain things in order to grow and thrive. Because the vine is rooted to the ground it has to take everything it needs from the soil, where the roots are, or the air where the leaves are.
A vine needs five things: warmth, sunlight, carbon dioxide, water and nutrients. The sun provides the heat and sunlight. Without enough heat a vine cannot grow. This is why it becomes dormant in the winter. Sunlight has a very special role. It allows the vine to combine carbon dioxide gas, which is taken in by the leaves, and water, which is taken up by the roots, in order to produce sugars. This process is known as photosynthesis. The vine uses the sugars to power its growth and ripen its grapes. In order to grow healthy shoots, leaves and roots a vine also needs small amounts of nutrients that are taken up by the roots.
Grapes
Grapes are the fruit of the vine. Many different types of plant produce fruit. They do so in order to attract animals, which eat the fruit and spread its seeds.

Ripening — At the start of the ripening process, grapes have very high levels of acid, hardly any sugar and have an unattractive herbaceous flavour. During ripening, all these characteristics change. The grapes swell with water and become soft and fleshy. The acidity drops and the amount of sugar in the grape rises.
As a grape ripens the herbaceous flavours decrease and the signature flavours develop. For some white grapes, the aromas and flavours change from green fruit to stone fruit and tropical fruit as the level of ripeness increases. For black grapes the aromas and flavours change from fresh fruit to cooked fruit.
There is another important change that happens in black grapes. Chemical compounds called tannins can be found in the skins of black grapes and, during this stage, they become riper. tannins contribute to the mouthfeel of red wines.
A grape grower needs to make sure that the tannins are ripe at the time of harvest because if they are unripe they can taste bitter. See Chapter 1 for more details.

Extra ripening — If the grapes are left on the vine past the point when they might normally be harvested, they continue to change and develop into a state that can be described as extra-ripeness. The grapes can only be left on the vine if weather conditions are suitable. The weather needs to be dry (rain can lead to the development of fungal diseases) and remain warm enough for ripening to continue. During the early stages of extra-ripeness, the grapes develop exaggerated ripe aromas and accumulate a higher level of sugar. After a longer period, the grapes start to turn into raisins because the water content in the grapes evaporates. This concentrates the acids and sugars in the grape and dried-fruit aromas start to develop. These grapes can have such high levels of sugar when they are harvested that they are commonly used to make sweet wines.

Botrytis/noble rot — Botrytis is a fungus that can grow on ripe grapes causing noble rot. It makes tiny holes in the skin of the grape, which causes the water inside the grape to evaporate. This concentrates the acids, sugars and flavours of the grape. The fungus can easily destroy grapes and the right conditions are needed if it is to be beneficial. First, the fungus must grow on ripe grapes. Second, the ideal conditions in the vineyard are damp misty mornings to allow the growth and spread of the fungus, followed by warm, dry afternoons to limit the growth of the fungus and prevent it from destroying the grapes. Grapes affected by noble rot are nearly always used to make sweet wines,

Frozen grapes — In some grape-growing regions, sugar in the grapes is concentrated in a very different way. Healthy grapes are left on the vine unpicked through autumn and into winter. When the temperature drops sufficiently, the water in the grapes freezes. The grapes are picked while frozen and are pressed before they thaw. When the frozen grapes are pressed in the winery, the ice crystals, along with the skins, can be separated from the small amount of remaining unfrozen liquid. This liquid contains high concentrations of acids, sugars and flavours. The resulting wines, known as ice wines, are sweet with concentrated and pure varietal characteristics.

Environmental influences affecting how vines grow and grapes ripen
Climate
Climate describes the average levels of temperature, sunlight and rainfall that a region can typically expect. It is essentially the long-term pattern of weather. While, unlike weather, climate does not change on a yearly basis, changes to climate can occur over a period of decades. Climate records show a sustained increase in average air and ocean temperatures that is predicted to continue. Greater climate variability and an increased incidence of extreme events such as droughts and floods are also currently being witnessed. Such changes are having and will continue to have a significant impact on grape growing. An increasing number of producers and industry bodies are taking action to adapt to and mitigate the effects of climate change.
One of the most important ways of classifying climate is by the amount of heat available during the growing season. Note, the growing seasons happen at different times in the northern and southern hemispheres. In the northern hemisphere it runs from April to October; in the southern hemisphere it runs from October to April.
There are many different ways to classify temperature. In this book we will use the following definitions:
Cool — Average growing season temperature of 16.5°C (62°F) or below
Moderate — Average growing season temperature of 16.5°C (62°F) to 18.5°C (65°F)
Warm — Average growing season temperature of 18.5°C (65°F) to 21°C (70°F)
temperature is so important because it determines what grape varieties can grow successfully in a wine region as well as the flavour characteristics they can develop. Grenache is an example of a vine variety that can only successfully grow and ripen its grapes in regions with a warm climate. On the other hand, some varieties can grow well in different climates but the flavours that develop in the grape are different depending on the climate. Chardonnay is a good example of this. See Chapter 10 for more details.
Climatic influences
Latitude — The most important factor that influences a wine region’s climate is latitude. The closer a region is to the equator, the hotter it is likely to be.
Most vineyards are located between 30° and 50° north or south of the equator.
Vineyards are rarely located outside of this range as it is likely to be either too hot or too cold for vines to survive.
However, there are other factors apart from latitude that can affect the climate of a region or a vineyard.

Altitude — Temperatures drop with altitude. A vineyard that is located at a higher altitude will always have a cooler climate than a vineyard located at the same latitude but with a lower altitude.
Seas — regions with warm climates that are located close to the sea can benefit from a moderating influence. South Africa is a good example of this. The climate in the grape-growing regions of the Cape is cooled by cold ocean currents.
Conversely, the vineyards of northern Europe benefit from the warming influence of warm ocean currents.
Rivers — Rivers take longer to warm up and cool down than land. In Autumn, rivers are warmer than the surrounding land. They provide warmth in cooler regions, helping to protect against frosts and extending the period over which it is sufficiently warm to ripen grapes. They can also reflect sunlight, which can help grapes to ripen in latitudes further from the equator.
Air — Cool air from seas and mountains has a moderating effect on the climate.
Warmer coastal areas can benefit from the effect of cool winds blowing on shore from cold seas. Cold air from mountains can move down to lower altitudes at night, cooling down warmer vineyards where heat can build up during the day.

Cloud, fog and mist — Cloud, fog and mist are water vapour that can be seen in the air. Clouds form above ground level and can block sunlight to a vineyard. The grapes take longer to ripen because the lack of sunlight slows down photosynthesis. This is a very important moderating factor in some warmer Australian regions.
Fog forms at ground level and cools down a vineyard. Fog provides a very important moderating influence in coastal regions of California and Chile.
Mist is a less dense form of fog. Mists that form near rivers during the mornings in autumn are essential for the development of botrytis/noble rot.
Mountains — Mountains can act as a barrier, sheltering vineyards from clouds, rain and cold winds. The protection given by mountains results in sunny summers, dry autumns and an extended growing season.
Slope and aspect — Vineyards that are further from the equator receive less heat and sunlight than vineyards located nearer the equator. The lack of light and heat in vineyards furthest away from the equator can make it challenging to grow and ripen grapes. However, if a vineyard is planted on a slope facing the equator it will receive more heat and light. These sloped sites will produce riper grapes than vineyards planted on flat land or on slopes facing away from the equator.
Soil — The soil provides the vine with water and nutrients. The soil therefore needs to store enough water and have enough nutrients to keep the vine healthy. The soil can also affect the temperature in the vineyard. If there are many stones on the surface of the soil, they can absorb heat and make the vineyard warmer. This extra heat can help with grape ripening.
Weather
Climate describes the average levels of temperature, sunlight and rainfall that a region can typically expect. Weather describes what actually happens in a region on a day-to-day basis. Weather extremes can cause problems for the grape grower,
Cool vintages - If the temperature in the growing season is colder than normal and the sunlight is less bright, the vines may struggle to ripen their grapes. Acid levels in the grapes will be too high and the sugar levels will be too low. The grapes may also struggle to develop their signature aromas.
Hot vintages — If the temperature during the growing season is hotter than normal and the sunlight is untypically bright, grapes can develop uncharacteristic driedfruit aromas and they can even be damaged by sunburn. In extreme conditions, the vines will also struggle to ripen their grapes. When it is hot, the vine needs more water. However, high temperatures are normally associated with low levels of rainfall and this presents the vines with a problem. When short of water, vines shut down to protect themselves and the grapes do not develop or ripen. In the most extreme conditions the vines can die. Irrigation can help, but in these conditions there is often no water available for irrigation.

Drought — Drought is caused by prolonged periods of below-average rainfall and is often made worse when there are above-average temperatures. In these conditions, the vine will be unable to ripen its grapes, and in the worst cases it may die. Drought can be overcome by using irrigation, but this is not always possible due to the lack of water.
High levels of rain — This can cause a grape grower many problems. The high levels of moisture can help fungal diseases to spread (see section Managing weeds, pests and diseases). If there is heavy rainfall close to harvest, the excessive moisture in the soil can cause the grapes to swell, diluting their flavours.
Hail — Hail can cause significant damage to vines and grapes. Heavy hail storms have the potential to destroy a vineyard's entire annual crop of grapes.
Frost — Frosts are a particular risk in the spring because they can damage and kill the vine's new growth. This reduces the number of grapes that can be grown in that year.
Growing grapes
Grape growers actively manage the vines they are responsible for throughout the year.
Training and pruning
Training and pruning are necessary in order to ensure that a healthy and commercially viable crop of grapes can be harvested successfully every year. The majority of vines are trained on trellises. Vines are typically pruned in the winter while they are dormant. Pruning ensures that individual vines retain their required shape.
Irrigation
In regions where there is a lack of rainfall, grape growers are normally allowed to irrigate their vines. There are a number of ways in which this can be done, from flooding the field to putting in place systems that are able to water individual vines.
Water is an increasingly limited and expensive resource; most grape growers now only use it sparingly.
Managing weeds, pests and diseases
Vines are vulnerable to many fungal diseases. The fungi grow on the grapes and the leaves and can reduce the quality and quantity of grapes that are produced.
Sometimes the damage that is caused is very significant. Small insects and other larger pests are another major problem that grape growers have to manage.

Larger animals and birds can be kept away from the grapes using nets and fences, but fungal diseases and insects are very hard to control. One option is to use chemical sprays. Sprays called fungicides are used to kill fungi and insecticides are used to kill insects. Weeds can also be a problem in some vineyards and herbicides can be used to keep these under control.
Some of these chemicals can cause longer-term problems in vineyards and many grape growers try to minimise their use. If a vineyard is certified as organic, then the grapes are grown without the use of most chemicals. However, some sprays are still permitted to keep the worst of the fungal diseases under control.
Yield
If a vine has sufficient heat, sunlight, water, nutrients and carbon dioxide it will be able to produce a commercial crop of ripe grapes. The yield is the measure of the amount of fruit produced per unit of area. In some regions the maximum yield allowed is set in law. If yields are too high the grapes could have diluted flavours and lower levels of sugar. If too few grapes are grown, the crop will be too small to be commercially viable,
Harvest
The timing of the harvest is an important choice that the grape grower has to make. If grapes are harvested earlier in the ripening process, they will have lower levels of sugar, higher levels of acid and less-ripe flavours. If they are harvested later in the ripening process, they will have higher levels of sugar, lower levels of acid and are likely to have riper flavours. Grapes that are left on the vine for a very long period of time start to shrivel. This process further concentrates grape sugars and the grapes can develop raisin and dried-fruit flavours.
Grapes can either be harvested by hand or by machine. Neither of these approaches is fundamentally better for wine quality than the other. The choice normally depends on the style of wine being made, although machine harvesters cannot be used on steep slopes.

Geographical Indications
Geographical Indications (Gls) are common to all the world’s wine-producing regions and regularly appear on wine labels. This is because the area where grapes are grown can have an influence on the style of the wine that is made.
A Gl is a legally defined vineyard area. These areas can be very large covering an entire region or in some instances an entire country, or they can be very small and be no more than a single vineyard. Not every wine comes from a Gl, though some statement of origin is legally required. For example, where applicable, the country of origin will appear on the label.
There are important differences between the Gls found in the European Union (EU)
and the rest of the world.

Other terms relating to grape-growing
Vine age
Older vines typically produce smaller numbers of grapes. The smaller crop means the grapes can have more concentrated and complex aromas, which can result in higher-quality wines. Older vines are therefore often highly prized by winemakers, and the wines made from their grapes are often labelled ‘Old Vine’ The French for old vines is vieilles vignes and this is sometimes seen on wine labels. However, this is not a legally defined concept and so there can be disagreement over the use of these terms.
Vintage
The vintage stated on a label refers to the year in which the grapes were harvested. Consumers can use this information to help decide when it is best to consume a wine. Most wines are best drunk when young. In this case, the vintage date indicates how old a wine is and whether or not it is past its best. Some wines are made in a style that benefits from an extended period of bottle ageing during which time they develop extra levels of complexity. In this case the vintage date can be used to indicate whether a wine is likely to be at its best.
In some regions, the weather can vary considerably from year to year and this can have a significant effect on the quality of the grapes. This can be an important consideration when buying very expensive wines from prestigious regions. It can help you to know whether a wine is different in style from what might be expected and decide if it is worth the retail price.
When locking at a vintage date it is important to note whether the country of origin is in the northern or southern hemisphere. A wine from Australia labelled 2022, for example, will be half a year older than a wine from California labelled 2022.
Late Harvest
Wines labelled ‘Late Harvest’ are made from grapes that have not been harvested at the normal time but have been left on the vine and picked much later. This results in grapes with riper flavours and higher sugar levels. They produce wines that are fuller-bodied and more concentrated in style compared with wines made from grapes harvested at the normal time. Late-harvest wines can be dry, off-dry, medium or sweet.
Botrytis/Noble Rot
This indicates that the grapes have been affected by noble rot. This results in grapes that have very highly concentrated levels of sugar and acid. Grapes affected by noble rot are nearly always used to make sweet wines.

Winemaking is the process that changes grape juice into wine. The most obvious difference between the two is that wine has alcohol, but this is not the only difference. The various options that are available to a winemaker are used to create a large number of different wine styles.
Winemaking processes
Common winemaking techniques
Crushing — This involves splitting the grape skins to release some of the grape juice.
Pressing — Crushed grapes are squeezed in a machine called a press in order to extract as much liquid as possible. This can happen either before or after fermentation, as explained in this chapter.
Alcoholic fermentation — Alcoholic fermentation is the process used to create alcohol and is the one thing that is common to all wines. It is carried out by microorganisms called yeast. The yeast feed on the sugars in the grape juice and produce alcohol. The yeast also produce carbon dioxide gas (CO,), which is allowed to escape into the atmosphere, and heat. Typically, the alcoholic fermentation stops naturally when the yeast have consumed all of the sugar in the grape juice.
This results in a dry wine. The level of alcohol depends on how much sugar there is in the grapes. alcohol levels in most dry wines are between 11.5% abv up to
16% abv. Medium and sweet wine production is covered later in this chapter.
Storage or maturation — Once the alcoholic fermentation has finished, the newly made wine is stored for a period of time prior to bottling. For most wines, this storage period is less than a year and, in some cases, only a few months. During such a short period of storage, the flavours in the wine change very little, if at all.
Some wines are stored for an extended period of time. This is commonly referred to as maturation. Over time, the flavours of the wine develop from primary to tertiary, and structural components such as tannins may become more integrated. Maturation normally takes place in vessels made from oak, these can sometimes add their own, secondary, flavours (see section Winemaking options later in this chapter).
Packaging — Glass bottles are still the most popular form of packaging used for wine. Bag-in-box, plastic bottles and bricks (such as Tetra Pak) are other options.

Making dry red wines
Red wines must be made from black grapes. The skins of these grapes must be included in the fermentation in order to extract their colour and tannins. Once alcoholic fermentation is finished, the newly made wine is drained from the skins and any remaining wine is extracted using a press.

Extracting colour and tannin
The heat created by yeast during fermentation helps to extract colour and tannin in red winemaking. However, on its own, this would be insufficient. This is because the grape skins float to the top of the fermenting grape juice forming a thick layer called the cap. If the cap is left there undisturbed it is not possible to extract much colour and tannin from it. The grape skins need to be frequently and thoroughly mixed together with the fermenting liquid. There are several techniques that can be used. Two of the most common are punching down and pumping over,
Punching down — In this technique a plunger is used to push the cap down into the liquid.
Pumping over — Liquid from the bottom of the fermentation vessel is pumped up through a hose and sprayed over the cap.

Making dry rose wines
There are several ways to make a rose wine. Two of the most common methods are short maceration and blending.
Short maceration
In this method, rosés are made from black grapes. The alcoholic fermentation starts in exactly the same way as in red winemaking, but in this instance the fermenting wine is drained from the skins after a few hours. Fermentation continues at white winemaking temperatures.
Blending
A rosé wine can also be made by blending a red wine with a white wine.
This method is not permitted in many parts of Europe.

Making sweet wines
Several white, red and rosé wines are not dry and contain sugar. The sugar that is left in a wine after fermentation is often referred to as residual sugar. The amount of residual sugar in these wines can vary considerably. Off-dry wines have a small amount of sugar; sweet wines can have extremely high levels of sugar. There are several different techniques that can be used to make these wines.
Concentrated grape sugars
The juice extracted from extra-ripe grapes, botrytis-/noble-rot-affected grapes and frozen grapes has such high levels of concentrated sugar that it is generally not possible for the yeast to eat all the sugar, therefore the fermentation stops with some residual sugar remaining. An example of a wine made this way is Tokaji Aszu from Hungary.
Removing the yeast
This technique requires the use of extremely sophisticated filters that have small enough holes to remove the yeast from the fermenting grape juice. An example of a wine made using this technique is White Zinfandel from California.
Killing the yeast
There are many ways this could be done, but today the most commonly used method is to add alcohol to the fermenting grape juice. This technique, called fortification, raises the level of alcohol to between 15-20% abv. This level of alcohol kills the yeast before they have fermented all the sugars. There is no risk that these wines might continue to ferment because of the high alcohol levels.
An example of a wine made this way is Port from Portugal.
Adding sweetness to a dry wine
This is a widely used technique that is popular with winemakers because they can precisely control the level of sweetness. Red, white and rosé wines are made this way.
The sugar that is added comes from grapes. Sometimes unfermented grape juice is used. Alternatively, the sugars in grape juice can be concentrated, resulting in a very sweet liquid that can be used to sweeten wines. The other option available to the winemaker is to blend a dry wine with a sweet wine,

Winemaking options
Aside from these common winemaking processes, there are several options that a winemaker can choose from in order to influence the style of the wine they are making.
Adjustments
Sometimes the grapes do not have the right proportion of components that the winemaker is looking for. Winemakers may therefore make small adjustments to the amount of sugar and acid in the grape juice.
Sugar - In cool climates or cool vintages the level of sugars in grapes can be too low. The wines made from these grapes are low in alcohol and lacking in body. It is possible to increase the level of sugar in the grape juice. This results in a wine with a higher level of alcohol.
Acid — In warm climates the acid levels in the grapes can become very low during ripening. Wines made from these grapes would be unbalanced due to low acidity.
To avoid this problem, a winemaker can add acid during winemaking.
In cool climates the level of acid in the grapes can be too high. In this instance the winemaker can neutralise some of the acid so that the wine is not unbalanced by high acidity.

Temperatures during the alcoholic fermentation
Alcoholic fermentation creates heat. The winemaker must monitor the temperature of the fermentation to ensure it is at a suitable level for the style of wine being made.
Winemakers today have access to many technologies that allow them to either chill or warm a fermentation so that it stays within the required temperature range.
Red wines — Red wines are typically fermented at higher temperatures than white wines. Temperatures range from 20°C to 32°C (68°F to 90°F). These higher temperatures are needed to help extract colour and tannins from the grape skins.
Rosé wines — If a rose wine is being made by short maceration then, once it has been drained off the skins, it is typically fermented at temperatures similar to those used for a white wine fermentation.
White wines — White wines are fermented at temperatures ranging from 12°C to
22°C (54°F to 72°F). At the lower end of this range, fermentations can produce notable fruity aromas.
Winery vessels
Winery vessels come in a range of shapes and sizes and can be made from different materials. It can be helpful to divide these into vessels made from an inert material and vessels made from oak.
stainless steel or concrete vessels — These vessels can be used for both fermentation and storage. They are both inert and do not add any flavour to the wine. They can also be made to be airtight, therefore preventing oxygen from interacting with the wine and causing the flavours to change.

Oak vessels — Wooden vessels can also be used for both fermentation and storage. Most are made out of ocak. When it comes to wine style, there are two reasons why a winemaker would use oak vessels (commonly known as ‘barrels’).
First, oak can add flavours directly to the wine and second, it can also allow flavours to evolve as a result of the interaction of the wine with oxygen during maturation.
If a winemaker wants to add oak flavours this will most commonly be done using a barrel. The amount of flavour a barrel can give a wine depends on how the barrel has been produced as well as the age and size of the barrel.
When a barrel is made, the oak staves that form the barrel are heated around an open flame and bent into shape. The length of time and the level of heat applied influence the flavours from the oak. This is referred to as the level of toasting.
Toasting can produce sweet-spice and charred wood aromas and flavours.
If a barrel is new and has not been used before it can add a great deal of flavour to the wine. These flavours can include vanilla, coconut, charred wood and spice.
These barrels are referred to as ‘new-oak’ barrels. If a wine is put into a barrel that has been used before, the barrel will add less flavour to the wine compared with a new-oak barrel. After two or three uses barrels have very little flavour left to give the wine. Barrels that have been used before are often referred to as ‘old-oak’ barrels.
Oak vessels are watertight, but they are not airtight. During maturation, oxygen from the atmosphere can enter the barrel and interact with the wine. This interaction with oxygen can cause flavours such as caramel, dried fruit, and nut
(hazelnut, almond, walnut) to develop. Oxygen helps to soften the tannins in red wines. These changes occur more quickly in small barrels compared with larger oak vessels because more of the surface area of the wine is in contact with the barrel. Note that large oak vessels are rarely used to give oak flavours to a wine.

Qak alternatives
Oak barrels are expensive and so if winemakers want to add oak flavours to less expensive wines, they are able to use other options. Oak staves or oak chips are much cheaper than oak barrels. They can be added to a wine that is stored in an inert vessel giving the wine oak flavours.
Malolactic conversion
Malolactic conversion takes place after the alcoholic fermentation has finished. It is not carried out by yeast but rather a different microorganism: bacteria. The activity of the bacteria lowers the acidity in the wine and can give buttery flavours.
In red winemaking, malolactic conversion nearly always takes place and the buttery flavours are not really noticeable. However, during white winemaking, the winemaker has a choice. Sometimes a winemaker may want to preserve the acidity in the wine and may feel that buttery aromas would not be suitable for the style of wine being made. therefore, they prevent malolactic conversion from taking place. This is a common choice for wine made from aromatic grape varieties such as Riesling. Other grape varieties can benefit from the influence of malolactic conversion, for example certain styles of Chardonnay.
Lees
When making white wines there is another choice that the winemaker must make. After alcoholic fermentation, the yeast die and fall to the bottom of the fermentation vessel, forming a layer of dead cells that is called ‘lees’. If the winemaker chooses to keep the wine in contact with the lees, they can be stirred up through the wine during maturation. This can increase the body of the wine and add extra flavours (biscuit, bread).
Blending
Blending can take place at many stages of the winemaking process and is important in defining and maintaining the style of a wine. Consumers generally expect some branded wines to taste the same from one year to the next. A
winemaker may therefore need to blend different wines each vintage to achieve the consistency required to maintain the brand's style. Another reason for blending is complexity. A winemaker can blend wines made from different grape varieties, from different vineyard locations or made by different techniques to create a wine that is more complex than its individual components.

Clarification
The vast majority of customers expect their wines to be perfectly clear. Techniques such as passing the wine through a filter can help achieve this. Some winemakers, however, feel that some of these techniques, including filtration, can take something away from the character of their wine, and therefore, choose not to use them. The resulting wines can sometimes be less than perfectly clear in the glass.
Maturation in the bottle
Once in-bottle most wines do not improve. Their fruit flavours start to fade and are replaced with vegetal notes. Some wines are able to mature and improve over the course of several years and sometimes decades. These wines often have high acidity and, in the case of sweet wines, high levels of residual sugar, and, in the case of red wines, high levels of tannins. These components of the wine help to preserve it. Above all else, wines that mature in a bottle need to have a sufficient concentration of flavours that can evolve in a positive way and add complexity to a wine, Fresh fruit flavours change to dried fruits, and other complex flavours, such as mushroom and leather, develop.
Changes to white wines
The colour of a white wine will change over time from lemon to gold to amber.
The intensity of the colour will also become deeper. With bottle age, tertiary characteristics can develop such as dried apricot, honey, nut and spice.
Changes to red wines
The colour in a red wine will change from ruby to garnet to tawny and, eventually, brown. The colour will also typically become paler in intensity. The tannins become softer and smoother. Tertiary characteristics such as fig, prune, meat and wet leaves can develop. Deposits can often form in bottles of red wine as they mature,
If a large amount of deposit has formed, the wine will normally be decanted before it is served.

section 3: Grape Varieties and Still Wines > 6 Pinot Noir
Pinot Noir is not an easy variety for growers to cultivate, but its wines are highly sought after. Most Pinot Noirs are fruity, refreshing and enjoyable to drink when young. The finest examples improve with long maturation and can develop complex flavours after many years in the bottle.
The characteristics of Pinot Noir
Pinot Noir is a black grape variety with a thin skin. It requires a cool to moderate climate in order to produce good-quality grapes. If the growing conditions are too warm, the grapes can lose their fresh fruit flavours and taste unattractively cooked.
Pinot Noir is prone to rot and disease. In poor vintages it can be a struggle to produce even small amounts of good-quality Pinot Noir.
As a wine, Pinot Noir is usually pale to medium in colour. On the palate it is high in acidity with low to medium tannins. It has red-fruit flavours (strawberry, raspberry, red cherry) that can range from fresh to cooked depending on the ripeness of the grapes.
Winemaking and maturation options
Pinot Noir is most commonly produced as a single-varietal wine, with the notable exception of sparkling wines such as Champagne. It is often matured in oak, but strong flavours from small new-oak barrels can easily overwhelm a delicately flavoured Pinot Noir. Older and/or larger barrels are often used as they impart less flavour. The best Pinot Noirs often have subtle oak-derived aromas (smoke, cloves, vanilla) that are balanced by the fruit flavours.
Although most Pinot Noirs are intended to be consumed while they are youthful and fruity, some very good or outstanding examples can develop complex tertiary flavours (forest floor, mushroom) in the bottle and may take several years to show their full potential.

California — Much of California is too warm for Pinot Noir, but there are a small number of regions close to the coast that produce very good or outstanding examples of Pinot Noir. New Zealand
Pinot Noir is the premier black grape variety of New Zealand. It is well suited to the cool climates of Martinborough and Marlborough that each produce very good and outstanding examples.
Despite its southerly latitude, Central Otago produces New Zealand's ripest and most powerfully flavoured Pinot Noir. The region is protected from sea breezes and rains by a ring of mountains. Pinot Noir today
Pinot Noir is renowned for its ability to express small changes in the vineyards that it comes from. The most experienced red Burgundy enthusiasts, for example, claim to be able to detect subtle differences between wines from vineyard plots just metres away from one another. For the more casual consumer, Pinot Noir provides a very refreshing red wine that can be happily consumed with or without food and rarely needs extended periods of bottle ageing before it is enjoyable to drink.
Pinot Noir is rarely an inexpensive wine but its continued popularity and supply and demand issues have caused it to be planted in regions that are too warm for quality production. This has resulted in some overly alcoholic wines entering the global market that lack identifiable Pinot Noir fruit flavours to balance them. The quality difference between these wines and those from cool or moderate sites is notable and truly demonstrates the importance of matching a variety with its favoured climate.

Section 3; Grape Varieties and Still Wines > 8 Riesling
Riesling is a cold-hardy white grape variety that prefers cool to moderate climates.
With its naturally high acidity and susceptibility to botrytis/noble rot, it is suitable for the production of outstanding-quality wines at all sweetness levels. Today, Riesling is produced across the world in a variety of styles.
Characteristics of Riesling
Riesling is an aromatic grape variety that produces wines with pronounced fruity and floral aromas and high acidity.
The specific fruit flavours in the wine vary according to the level of ripeness in the grapes at the time of harvest. Grapes that are just-ripe produce wines with green fruit (apple, pear), citrus (lemon, lime) and floral (blossom) characteristics. Extra-ripe grapes give flavours of stone fruit (peach, apricot), tropical fruit (mango, pineapple)
and sometimes even dried fruit (dried apricots).
Styles of Riesling range from dry to sweet and from light to full in body. As a variety that retains acidity and builds up sugar and flavour slowly, Riesling is suitable for late-harvesting in regions where there are dry, sunny autumn conditions. A grape's sugars, acidity and flavours can be concentrated by late-harvesting, through effect of botrytis or as a result of the grapes freezing on the vine. Riesling’s high natural acidity is perfect for balancing high levels of sweetness. See Chapter 5 for more information on sweet winemaking.
Winemaking and maturation options
Riesling produces dry, off-dry, medium and sweet white wines. Off-dry or medium styles are usually made by interrupting the fermentation by removing the yeast, which leaves a wine with lower alcohol and some residual sugar, or by the addition of unfermented grape juice (Sissreserve). The sweetest styles are made using extra-ripe grapes, the grape juice is so high in sugar that the fermentation stops naturally.

France
The French region of Alsace AOC lies to the east of the Vosges Mountains, which protect the vineyards from rain-bearing westerly winds. It is therefore one of the driest and sunniest wine-producing regions in all of France. The best vineyards are planted in the mountain foothills facing east or south-east to benefit from the morning sun. Dry autumns extend the period over which the grapes can ripen.

The best wines come from individual vineyards called grands crus. Sweeter styles are also made from late-harvested grapes. These wines are labelled
Vendanges tardives. See Chapter 12 for more information on labelling terms used in Alsace.
Australia
Australia has been cultivating Riesling vines since the early 19th century. Premium

Due to their higher altitude, these two regions have moderate climates, in contrast to the nearby warm Barossa Valley. They produce refreshing Rieslings in a dry style with pronounced aromas of lime, lemon and, sometimes, petrol (gasoline).
Riesling today
Riesling reaches its potential in wine regions that are cool, dry and sunny enough to allow its sugars and flavours to develop slowly. Beyond the regions that have been mentioned in this chapter, several other cool to moderate regions around the world have had success with this grape variety.
Despite its widespread plantings, Riesling continues to be widely misunderstood by the global consumer. Its German roots mean that it is often incorrectly linked to low-quality sweetened wines known as Liebfraumilch, a style that has now fallen out of fashion. Despite this, many wine enthusiasts hold Riesling in very high regard due to its versatility, flavour intensity and ability to age. It is therefore the premium-guality wines that continue to drive sales of Riesling worldwide.

Chardonnay is a white grape variety that is prized for its ability to produce outstanding-quality wines in cool, moderate and warm climates. It produces a broad range of dry white wines, from inexpensive high-volume brands to premium-guality wines that can evolve for years in the bottle.
The characteristics of Chardonnay
The flavours that can be found in Chardonnay wines are many and varied. Its primary fruit aromas and the degree of alcohol, body and acidity are all greatly affected by the climate in which the grapes are grown.
A typical cool-climate Chardonnay has high acidity, light to medium body and flavours of green fruits (apple, pear), citrus fruits (lemon) and wet stones.
A typical moderate-climate Chardonnay has medium to high acidity, medium to full body and flavours of lemon, stone fruits (peach) and sometimes tropical fruit (melon).
A typical warm-climate Chardonnay is full-bodied, medium in acidity and dominated by flavours of stone fruit (peach) and tropical fruit (pineapple, banana).
Winemaking and maturation options
Acidification, the process of adding extra acid during winemaking, is commonplace for many warm-climate Chardonnays to balance the alcohol and body of the wine.
See Chapter 5 for more information on wine adjustments.
Alongside its varied primary fruit flavours, it is common to taste secondary flavours from various winemaking processes in wines made from Chardonnay. Malolactic conversion can add dairy flavours (butter, cream) and soften harsh acids. Lees contact during maturation can add body and flavours of bread and biscuit. Oak barrel fermentation and/or maturation is also commonly used to add body and flavours (smoke, vanilla, coconut). Some winemakers may use oak chips or staves to impart these flavours as they are less expensive than new-oak barrels.

It is important to note that the above methods are all optional. Strong secondary flavours can easily overwhelm lightly flavoured wines; only wines of sufficient flavour intensity will benefit from these winemaking processes. Some producers prefer not to add secondary flavours to their Chardonnay. They use inert vessels and may avoid malolactic conversion and/or lees contact. Wines made without oak treatment may be labelled ‘unoaked’ or ‘unwooded’ to communicate this to the consumer.
Very good or outstanding-quality Chardonnays can age in the bottle for years, gathering complex tertiary flavours (hazelnut, mushroom).

In many markets, Chardonnay has suffered from its association with heavy oak flavours, which were common in the Chardonnay wines of the 1980s and 1990s.

Sauvignon Blanc is an aromatic white grape variety that is best known for producing dry wines with distinctive herbaceous aromas and refreshing high acidity. Though it is considered to be a French variety, it is now widely grown in cool and moderate climates around the world. It produces wines that are most commonly unoaked and best consumed while young and fresh.
The characteristics of Sauvignon Blanc
Sauvignon Blanc is an aromatic white grape variety that grows best in cool and moderate climates. It typically produces dry wines that are light to medium in body with high acidity.
Its wines are characterised by their pronounced herbaceous aromas (grass, green bell pepper/capsicum, asparagus). Primary flavours vary according to ripeness — from green fruits (apple, gooseberry), citrus fruit (grapefruit) and wet stones, to riper flavours of stone fruit (peach) and tropical fruit (passion fruit).

Winemaking and maturation options
Sauvignon Blanc is usually seen as a single-varietal wine, though it is sometimes blended with Semillon.
Single-varietal wines made from Sauvignon Blanc are most commonly produced in inert vessels; their distinctive herbaceous characters may be easily overwhelmed by the flavours of new oak. If oak has been used during winemaking, it will typically be indicated by terms such as ‘barrel aged.
Wines made from Sauvignon Blanc are usually best consumed when young. Even outstanding examples can lose their fresh aromas within a couple of years of the vintage and develop unattractive vegetal notes.

Pinot Grigio and Pinot Gris are two names for the same white grape variety. Pinot
Grigio is the Italian name and Pinot Gris is the French name. It grows primarily in cool or moderate climates and produces two distinct styles that are determined principally by grape-growing and harvesting choices.
The characteristics of Pinot Grigio/Pinot Gris
Pinot Grigio/Pinot Gris is very versatile in the vineyard and produces two very distinct styles of wine. The most common style is made from high yields of grapes that are harvested early to retain acidity. The result is a dry, light-bodied wine with medium to high acidity and simple flavours of pear and lemon.
A very different style can be created when yields are controlled and the harvesting of the grapes is delayed. These riper grapes are higher in sugar, lower in acidity and more complex in flavour. From these grapes it is possible to produce a powerful, full-bodied wine with medium acidity and pronounced flavours of lemon, stone fruit (peach) and tropical fruit (mango, banana).
Winemaking and maturation options
Pinot Grigio/Pinot Gris is typically fermented in inert vessels to preserve its fruity flavours. The lighter Italian style is almost always fermented to dryness, while producers of the riper, fuller-bodied style sometimes interrupt the fermentation by removing the yeasts to retain an off-dry or medium level of sweetness.
Lighter styles of Pinot Grigio are best consumed while fresh and young, as their simple flavours fade within a short time in the bottle. The more concentrated fullbodied examples can develop notes of ginger and honey over time in the bottle.

These three white grapes create very distinctive single-varietal wines.
Gewurztraminer and Viognier are responsible for some of the world's fullestbodied and most floral white wines. Albarino is an increasingly fashionable grape variety known for refreshing wines with concentrated fruit flavours.
Gewurztraminer
Gewurztraminer is an aromatic variety that grows best in cool to moderate climates. It creates powerfully scented wines that range from dry to sweet in style.
Gewurztraminer grapes accumulate high levels of sugar during ripening. When the juice is fermented to dryness, the result is a high-alcohol wine. A typical
Gewurztraminer is full-bodied with low to medium acidity. It has pronounced floral aromas (rose) as well as stone-fruit (peach, apricot) and tropical-fruit (lychee)
flavours. Gewurztraminer is typically unoaked to preserve its floral aromas. Very good or outstanding examples can develop flavours of honey and dried fruit in the bottle.
The most famous region for Gewurztraminer is Alsace AOC in France, where it produces some of the region’s highest-quality grand cru wines alongside Riesling and Pinot Gris.
Gewurztraminer’s pronounced and distinctive aromas make it a popular choice with consumers who enjoy complex and flavourful white wines.
Viognier
Viognier is an aromatic grape variety that is mainly grown in moderate climates.
A typical Viognier is dry with low to medium acidity, high alcohol and a medium to full body. Its wines have pronounced floral aromas (blossom) and stone-fruit flavours (peach, apricot). Most wines are fermented in inert vessels but, unusually for an aromatic variety, some oak-aged Viogniers are made.

Albarino
Albarifio is a white grape variety that is grown in the Rias Baixas DO region in the north-west corner of Spain. Close to the Atlantic Ocean, this region has a moderate climate and is notably cooler, wetter and cloudier than many of Spain's other wine-producing regions. This means that it is well suited to the production of refreshing white wines.
A typical Albarifio is dry with high acidity. It is medium-bodied with flavours of citrus fruit (lemon, grapefruit) and stone fruit (peach, apricot). Albarifio is most commonly an unoaked wine that is intended to be drunk when young. Sometimes a small amount of lees contact is used to add body and complexity.
Albarifio has also enjoyed a recent increase in popularity and producers outside of Spain are beginning to plant this variety in suitable sites around the world.

Section 3: Grape Varieties and Still Wines > 14 Merlot
From its homeland in Bordeaux, Merlot has been exported and planted throughout the world. It is a very versatile grape that can produce light, fruity wines intended for immediate consumption as well as pronounced, concentrated wines that improve with oak and bottle maturation. It is often used to add an appealing softness to blends with higher-tannin black grape varieties, such as Cabernet Sauvignon.
The characteristics of Merlot
Merlot is a black grape variety that grows in moderate and warm climates.
A single-varietal Merlot is typically a dry wine with medium acidity and medium tannins. Its primary characters vary according to ripeness.
Just-ripe Merlot grapes produce light- to medium-bodied wines with fresh redfruit flavours (strawberry, red plum) and herbaceous notes (green bell pepper/
capsicum). Riper Merlot grapes can produce wines with medium to full body and cooked-black-fruit flavours (blackberry, black plum). This riper style is easily achieved in a warm climate but can also be achieved by harvesting later in a moderate climate.
Winemaking and maturation options
While it is capable of making outstanding single-varietal wines, Merlot is often blended with higher-tannin varieties such as Cabernet Sauvignon to achieve a particular style of wine. Merlot adds red-fruit flavours to these blends and can lower the tannin levels, making them ready to drink at an earlier stage.
Both caked and unoaked styles are made: while a lighter style of Merlot has little need for extended barrel maturation, a fuller-bodied style may benefit from the softness and flavour complexity gained through oak maturation. The best
Merlot-dominated wines are age-worthy, developing notes of dried fruit and tobacco over time.

Section 3: Grape Varieties and Still Wines > 15 Cabemet Sauvignon
Cabernet Sauvignon is a black grape variety from south-west France. It is notable for its pronounced black-fruit and herbaceous aromas, high tannins and high acidity. Due to these natural features, it can produce outstanding-quality red wines that mature and develop for many years in the bottle. It is the grape variety behind many of the most famous wines from the Left Bank of Bordeaux in France. Today, outstanding examples are made throughout the wine-producing world.
The characteristics of Cabernet Sauvignon
Cabernet Sauvignon grows in moderate and warm climates, though in a moderate climate it can struggle to ripen in all but the warmest sites. Its grapes have thick skins that contain high levels of colour, flavour and tannin. The grapes need a long and warm growing season to ripen fully.
A single-varietal Cabernet Sauvignon will be deeply coloured. On the palate it will be dry, high in acidity, high in tannins, and medium to full in body. It will have pronounced flavours of black fruit (blackcurrant, black cherry) and, usually, herbal
(mint) or herbaceous notes (green bell pepper/capsicum). In warm climates, its fruit flavours can become cooked in character.
Winemaking and maturation options
Cabernet Sauvignon can be found as a single-varietal wine, though it is often blended with other varieties such as Merlot. Blending can help to soften Cabernet
Sauvignon's very high levels of tannin and acidity. This may be particularly important in cooler vintages in moderate-climate regions, where Cabernet
Sauvignon may struggle to ripen.
A newly fermented Cabernet Sauvignon can taste unpleasantly tannic. It will often be matured in oak barrels for several months or even years before it is bottled.
Oak maturation allows small amounts of oxygen into the wine: this helps to soften the tannins. A new-oak barrel will also impart complex secondary flavours (smoke, vanilla, cloves, cedar).

Section 3: Grape Varieties and Still Wines > 16 Syrah/Shiraz
Syrah and Shiraz are two names for the same variety. ‘Syrah’ is the French name while ‘Shiraz’ is the name most commonly used in Australia. It is a black grape variety known for producing wines with black-fruit flavours and characteristic aromas of black pepper. It can produce soft and fruity wines meant for early drinking as well as exceptionally concentrated examples that evolve complex tertiary flavours in the bottle.
The characteristics of Syrah/Shiraz
Syrah/Shiraz is a thick-skinned black variety with small grapes. It requires a moderate to warm climate to ripen fully and produces wines with medium to high levels of tannins and acidity. A moderate-climate Syrah/Shiraz is typically medium in body with fresh black-fruit flavours (black cherry, blackberry), herbal notes and black-pepper aromas. This is the signature style of the Northern Rhone where it is known as Syrah. A warm-climate Syrah/Shiraz is typically full-bodied and high in alcohol with ripe flavours of cooked black fruits and liquorice. This riper style is common in the warmer parts of Australia where the wines are known as Shiraz.
Outside of France and Australia, the use of one name over the other can give the consumer an idea of which style the wine has been made in.
Winemaking and maturation options
Syrah/Shiraz is responsible for many very high-quality single varietal wines, but is also used to add colour, black-fruit flavours and tannins to blends. It is most commonly blended with grapes that are local to the Southern Rhéne such as Grenache (see
Chapter 18). These wines are sometimes referred to as ‘Rhone Blends’.
Oak maturation is common in the production of Syrah/Shiraz wines. It is used both to soften the tannins of the wine and to add flavours of smoke and spice. Very good or outstanding wines from Syrah/Shiraz are often suitable for bottle ageing due to their intense flavours and tannins. For the finest examples, this ageing period can extend to a decade or more. Over time, dried-fruit flavours emerge as well as notes of leather, meat and earth.

Section 3; Grape Varieties and Still Wines > 18 Grenache/Garnacha
This grape variety is thought to have evolved in Spain where it is called Garnacha.
From there it spread through the south of France and into the Rhéne Valley.
Once in France its name changed to Grenache and this is the name that is most commonly used in the rest of the world.
Grenache is a black grape variety that needs a warm climate to ripen successfully.
When ripe, Grenache grapes typically have high levels of sugar and low acidity.
It is a thin-skinned variety and its wines are typically low to medium in tannins and have red-fruit flavours (strawberry, red plum, red cherry) and sometimes spice
(white pepper, liquorice). Grenache produces its finest wines when yields are carefully controlled.
Due to its red-fruit flavours Grenache is commonly used to make rosé wines using the short maceration method. These wines can range from dry to medium in sweetness and are typically intended to be consumed chilled while young and fruity.
Grenache is often blended with other black varieties, such as Syrah in France and
Tempranillo in Spain. These varieties add colour, acidity and tannins to Grenachebased wines. Many simple Grenache-based wines are unoaked, but very good or outstanding-quality wines are often matured in barrels to add flavour complexity.
Some producers flavour old oak over new, due to Grenache’s delicate red-fruit flavours. Over time in the bottle, Grenache can develop complex flavours of earth, meat and dried fruit.

Northern Italy's vineyard regions are located in the foothills of the Alps and on the flat plains of rivers. The bubbles in sparkling wines are created by dissolved carbon dioxide gas (CO,).
They can be added by pumping carbon dioxide through a wine, but most premiumquality sparkling-wine producers harness the CO, that is naturally created as a byproduce of fermentation. Sparkling wines are most commonly produced using one of two methods: bottle fermentation or tank fermentation.
The base wine
The first step in producing nearly all styles of sparkling wine is to make a base wine.
It is typically dry, high in acidity and low in alcohol. The base wine is usually a blend of different wines: these can be from different vintages, varieties and/or vineyards.
Second fermentation
The CO, that makes the bubbles in most sparkling wines is a by-produce of a second fermentation. To instigate the second fermentation, sugar and yeast are added to the dry base wine. The yeast convert the sugar into alcohol and CO..
The alcohol that is created brings the abv of the wine up by around 1.5%.
To stop the CO, from escaping, the second fermentation must take place in a sealed vessel. This is typically either a sealed bottle (bottle fermentation) or a sealed tank (tank fermentation). The choice of vessel will depend on the style and flavour profile desired, as well as regional traditions or regulations.

Cava
Cava is the Spanish term for a traditional-method sparkling wine produced in certain regions of Spain. The vast majority of Cava comes from the Catalunya region. The grape varieties used to make Cava are mainly local Spanish varieties, although Chardonnay and Pinot Noir are also permitted and can add both fruit flavours and acidity to the base wine.
Catalunya’s warm climate means that the grapes may need to be harvested early to retain high acidity. The base wine typically has light flavours of apple and lemon.
After secondary fermentation, most Cava is aged on its lees for a shorter amount of time than Champagne and has very subtle autolytic characters.
Cava production is dominated by a small number of large brands who make high volumes of simple non-vintage wines. However, there are many smaller producers making very good-quality Cavas that are aged in contact with lees for extended periods.
South Africa, USA, Australia and New Zealand
In all of these places there are important producers of bottle-fermented sparkling wines. Usually it is the coolest regions that produce the highest-quality sparkling wines. Many producers choose to use the Champagne grape varieties
(Chardonnay and Pinot Noir) for their base wines. The best examples are intense and complex with a long finish. They typically have slightly riper fruit flavours than
Champagne due to the extra sunlight and warmth in the vineyards.
In South Africa, the labelling term Cap Classique is used to signify a traditionalmethod sparkling wine. Sometimes the Champagne grapes are used, other times they may use other varieties such as Chenin Blanc.
Tank fermentation
Tank method - dry wines
The tank method can be used if the winemaker does not wish to add autolytic flavours to their wine. This may be the case if the base wine is made from aromatic grape varieties.
The process is relatively simple. The base wine is put into a sealed tank along with yeast and sugar. The second fermentation takes place in the tank, creating bubbles. The lees are removed by filtration and the wine is bottled under pressure and sealed with a thick cork and a wire cage.
The result is a wine that still tastes predominantly of the base wine, without any autolytic character. This method is significantly less expensive and labour intensive than bottle fermentation.

The Asti method — sweet wines
This is a variation on the tank method. It is commonly referred to as the Asti method as it is used to make the sweet, low-alcohol sparkling wines of Asti DOCG
in Piemonte in north-west Italy. However, this method is used around the world to make wines in a similar style.
This method does not begin with a dry base wine. Instead, juice is put into a pressurised tank with yeast and begins to ferment. At first the CO, created is allowed to escape. Part way through the fermentation the tank is sealed so that the
CO, is retained, creating bubbles. The fermentation is stopped by filtering out the yeasts before all the sugar has been converted to alcohol. The resulting wine is low in alcohol, sweet and sparkling.
Tank-fermented sparkling wines
Prosecco DOC is a sparkling wine from north-east Italy. It is typically made using the tank method. The grape variety used to produce the base wine is Glera. Glera is a white variety that produces light- to medium-bodied wines with pear, melon and floral (blossom) aromas. Prosecco DOC is typically dry or off-dry. It comes in both white and rosé styles; the rosé wines are made predominantly from Glera with a small proportion of Pinot Noir to give the pink colour.
Asti DOCG is a sweet, fruity and light-bodied wine from Piemonte. It is made with the Moscato (Muscat) grape variety that gives pronounced floral aromas (blossom)
and flavours of grapes, peaches and pears. Asti DOCG is usually fully sparkling.
Tank-fermented sparkling wines are widely produced across the world. A popular trend is the production of fully or lightly sparkling wines from the Moscato/Muscat grape. These wines are typically sweet and low in alcohol, made using the Asti method.

Section 4: Other Styles of Wine > 25 Fortified Wines
Sherry and Port are fortified wines, which means that additional alcohol is added to the wine. Traditionally, fortification was used to strengthen wine to protect it from spoilage. All Port is sweet as it is made by interrupting the fermentation using fortification. Most Sherry is made in a dry style, but it is sometimes sweetened later on. Both Sherry and Port are made in a range of diverse styles.
Sherry production
Sherry is a fortified wine produced around the town of Jerez de la Frontera in southern Spain. There are many styles, but the starting point for almost all Sherry is a simple dry base wine made from Palomino, a local white grape variety.
Once fermentation is complete, alcohol is added to fortify the wine before it enters the solera system. A solera is a system of old-oak casks containing wines of different ages. The wines are continually blended together as they age, ensuring a consistent and complex style of mature wine. It is in the solera that Sherry develops its various distinctive flavours,
Sherry styles
Dry Sherry styles
The three most important styles of dry Sherry are Fino, Oloroso and Amontillado.
They are distinct from one another largely due to different ageing periods and conditions within the solera.
Fino Sherry ages under a thick white layer of yeast known as flor. This method of ageing under a layer of flor is known as biological ageing. The base wine is fortified to around 15% abv before it enters the solera. The flor forms on the surface of the wine and protects it from oxygen. A Fino is typically pale lemon in colour with aromas of apple, almonds and pronounced flavours derived from the flor
(bread dough). After bottling, these wines rapidly lose their freshness and should be consumed as early as possible. Fino Sherry is best served chilled.

Oloroso Sherry production does not involve flor. Instead, the dry base wine is fortified to around 17% abv — a strength at which the flor cannot survive, Without the flor's protection, the wine ages oxidatively — in contact with oxygen. Over time, the wine becomes brown in colour and develops flavours of dried fruits (raisins, prunes) and notes of deliberate oxidation (walnuts, caramel).
Amontillado Sherry is somewhere between a Fino and an Oloroso Sherry in style.
It ages under flor for a period of time before it is refortified to around 17% abw. This kills the flor and allows the wine to age oxidatively until it is bottled. Amontillado
Sherries are notably deeper in colour than Finos and have flavours both from flor
(bread dough) and from oxidative ageing (walnuts, caramel).

Sweet Sherry styles
Dry Sherries can be sweetened with a sweetening component (see Chapter 5)
but must be labelled with a different or supplementary term to signify this. The term Pale Cream signifies a sweetened Fino. The terms Medium and Cream are often used for sweetened Amontillado or Oloroso Sherries.
PX (Pedro Ximénez) is a sweet Sherry made from white Pedro Ximenez grapes that have been concentrated by sun-drying. The wine is fortified and aged oxidatively in the solera. The resulting wine is deep brown in colour, sweet with pronounced dried-fruit flavours (fig, prune, raisin), liquorice and molasses. Pedro
Ximénez Sherry is often used as the sweetening component in Cream Sherries.
Port production
Port is a sweet fortified wine produced from grapes grown in the Upper Douro region of Portugal. It is made from a blend of local black grape varieties; most Ports are blends of wines from several vintages.
All Port follows the same basic winemaking process. After the harvest, the first step is to rapidly extract colour and tannin from the skins of the grapes. Traditionally this is done by foot treading, though machines now exist for this purpose. Fermentation is interrupted by adding grape spirit. This kills the yeast, which stops the fermentation and results in a wine that is sweet and high in alcohol. The wine is then matured for a period of time prior to blending and bottling. As with Sherry, the maturation process plays an important role in determining the different styles of Port.
Port styles
Ruby-style Ports
Ruby-style Ports are deeply coloured and fruity. After fortification, they spend a period of time ageing in large vessels before they are bottled ready to drink.
The traditional vessels are large old-oak casks, though stainless steel tanks are sometimes used. Ruby-style wines typically have flavours of cooked black fruits
(black cherry, blackberry).
Inexpensive Ruby Ports are generally sweet, simple, fruity wines with lower tannins than other ruby-style Ports and often some red fruit character alongside the black fruits. Reserve Ruby Ports use better-quality wines with greater flavour intensity.
They are sometimes matured for longer than Ruby Ports to soften and integrate the added alcohol.
Late Bottled Vintage (LBV) Port is similar in style to a Reserve Ruby but contains wines from a single vintage.

Vintage Port
Vintage Port is made from the highest-quality wines from a single exceptional vintage. It is high in tannins and has very concentrated flavours. It has the potential to mature in-bottle for at least 20 years and sometimes much longer. Over time the colour turns from ruby to garnet and the wine develops complex tertiary aromas of dried fruit, leather and forest floor. A thick deposit forms and the wine needs to be decanted before serving. Vintage Ports are not made every year and Port producers only ‘declare’ a Vintage Port in the best years.
Tawny-style Ports
Tawny-style Ports are so called due to their tawny colour. The best of these wines develop their colour from extended oxidative ageing in small barrels. Over time, the wine becomes more and more brown in colour and it develops complex notes of dried fruit and deliberate oxidation (walnut, coffee, caramel). Wines made this way typically have an age indication of 10, 20, 30 or 40 years but Tawny Ports aged for even longer can be found. However, inexpensive wines labelled Tawny Port are simple, fruity, low in tannin and pale in colour.
